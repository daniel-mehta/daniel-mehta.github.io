# Robo-Advisors Can Democratize Investing, but Governance Matters
> 2026-06-23

I’ve been thinking about robo-advising in Canada, using [**Wealthsimple**](https://www.linkedin.com/company/wealthsimple/) as an example, and it challenged some of my assumptions.  
  
My original view was that robo-advisors are well-suited to routine portfolio management. They can provide scalable, lower-cost investing access based on a user’s goals, time horizon, financial situation, and risk tolerance.  
  
I still think that’s true.  
  
What changed is how I think about access.  
  
Traditional financial advice can be expensive, which means many households may not receive meaningful guidance at all. Robo-advisors can help lower that barrier by making basic investing and portfolio management more accessible.  
  
But there’s an important tension.  
  
The users who benefit most from lower-cost financial tools may also face the greatest information gap when trying to evaluate whether a recommendation is suitable, transparent, or aligned with their interests.  
  
That’s where governance matters.  
  
In low-stakes recommendation systems, a bad recommendation might mean ordering the wrong food or watching the wrong movie.  
  
In financial advice, a poor recommendation can affect someone’s long-term financial well-being, especially when financial literacy, income stability, debt, family obligations, or life circumstances make decisions more complex.  
  
So my view has evolved.  
  
I still see robo-advising as valuable for scalability and financial inclusion. But I increasingly see it as a complement to human advice rather than a full replacement.  
  
As AI systems take on more consequential decisions, suitability, transparency, privacy, and accountability become just as important as the technology itself.
