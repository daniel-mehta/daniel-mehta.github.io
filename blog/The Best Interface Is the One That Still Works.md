# The Best Interface Is the One That Still Works
> 2026-07-21


Critical digital services should not be designed only for normal conditions.  
  
They should be designed for the moments when demand spikes, networks slow down, and users need answers quickly.  
  
Power outage systems are a good example.  
  
During a major storm, most people do not need a complex interactive map first. They need a few reliable facts:  
  
Where is the outage?  
  
How many people are affected?  
  
Has a crew been assigned?  
  
When was the information last updated?  
  
What is the estimated restoration time?  
  
A better design could load a lightweight outage list by default, then offer the full map only when requested.  
  
That would reduce unnecessary data transfer and computation while also making the service easier to use on slow mobile connections.  
  
It could also improve trust by clearly showing whether information is live, delayed, or stale.  
  
This reflects a broader systems principle:  
  
The most impressive interface is not always the most useful one.  
  
For critical public services, resilience often comes from simplicity, progressive loading, clear status information, and keeping essential functions available when conditions are at their worst.  
  
Good systems are not just efficient when everything works.  
  
They remain useful when it doesn’t.
